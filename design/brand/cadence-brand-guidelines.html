01 — The Name

Cadence.

A single word that holds three meanings the product needs at once.

Cadence (n.) the rhythm of a runner's steps. The number of strides per minute that, when held steady, defines an athlete's efficiency. Every wearable in this product's category measures it. It is the most quietly important number in endurance sport.

Cadence (n.) the rhythm of speech. The pace and lilt of a voice that makes it pleasant to listen to. The product's AI assistant has a cadence — it is, literally, how it will be heard.

Cadence (n.) the rhythm of work. What gets diarised gets done. What gets analysed gets perfected. The product exists to give the user a steady, productive cadence across training, work, and life.

Why it works

  • Premium without being precious. Reads as a domain term, not a mascot.
  • Athletic without being gym-bro. Endurance sport, not weightlifting.
  • British and quiet. Three syllables, no shouting.
  • Unowned in the category. Whoop, Oura, Strava, Notion — Cadence sits alongside them cleanly.
  • Scales from one user to a public product without re-meaning.
  • Pronounceable, memorable, dictatable to Siri on the first try.

Wordmark

Set in lowercase Fraunces, optical size 144, with a moss-green terminal full stop. The lowercase reads as personal, the full stop reads as resolved.

Alternative names considered

Each viable; each tilts the brand a different way. Cadence remains the recommendation.

Mile.
02 — Declarative

Monosyllabic, blunt, British. Reads as a unit and an aspiration. Strongest in marketing copy — "Where will your mile take you?" — but slightly thinner conceptually for an AI assistant. The assistant is not a unit.

Tally.
03 — Quietly British

Has charm. A tally is what you keep — of runs, of meetings, of small daily wins. Risks sounding domestic against premium tech competitors (Linear, Superhuman). Use this if the brand wants to be warmer than Cadence allows.

Heron.
04 — Observational

A patient British wading bird that stands still and watches before it strikes. Beautiful conceptually — the assistant observes, then acts. Risk: animal mascots are crowded (Mailchimp, Twitter, Duolingo) and a bird may read as too lifestyle-blog.

Atlas.
05 — Cartographic

Knowing, holding-the-world, navigational. Strong for an assistant with full life context. Slightly overused: Atlas Obscura, Atlassian, Boston Dynamics' robot. Available domains are scarce.

02 — Foundations

What we are.

Mission

To give one person — and eventually anyone — the data, the discipline, and the daily companion to live and train at the level they choose.

Positioning

Cadence sits at the intersection of wearable-driven performance (Whoop, Strava, Oura), second-brain productivity (Notion, Linear, Superhuman), and conversational AI (Pi, Claude). It is the first product that does not sit in any one of those categories — it sits across them.

Promise

Two principles, taken from the founder, taken into the brand:

Product, in one sentence

A premium dashboard for the body, a calm assistant for the day, and one voice connecting both.

Audience, today and tomorrow

Today: one ultra-runner who runs a business and values friction-free interfaces above almost everything. Tomorrow: a small public of operators, athletes, and senior individual contributors who want the same.

What gets diarised gets done.
What gets analysed gets perfected.

These two lines are the entire brand. Every design decision — every chart, every microcopy line, every animation — should serve one of them or both. If it does neither, it should not ship.

04 — Logo System

The mark.

A wordmark, a monogram, and an app-icon mark. One family. No decoration.

Primary wordmark

Lowercase "cadence" set in Fraunces, weight 360, optical size 144, with a soft-axis setting of 60. The terminal full stop is filled with the brand's moss accent. The full stop is non-negotiable — it is what distinguishes the wordmark from the typeface and signals resolution, the quiet end of a sentence well-said.

Monogram & App-Icon Mark

A single character formed from two arcs and a void. It reads as a c, a metronome's swing, and a pair of footfalls. Sized to read clearly at favicon scale (16 × 16 px).

Monogram
On Moss

Clear space & minimum sizes

Clear space around any mark is equal to the height of the lowercase 'c' counter. Minimum reproduction size: 18px for the wordmark, 16px for the monogram. Below these sizes, use the monogram alone.

What not to do

  • Never set the wordmark in any other typeface, weight, or case.
  • Never tilt, skew, or stretch the wordmark or monogram.
  • Never remove the moss dot — it is part of the mark, not a decoration.
  • Never recolour the wordmark to anything other than ink, bone, or moss.
  • Never use the mark over a busy photograph without a solid hold-out.

How the accents earn their place

Accessibility

06 — Typography

Three families, three jobs.

Editorial display for headlines and meaningful numbers. A precise grotesque for interface. A monospaced face for data and metadata.

Fraunces

A contemporary editorial serif with a soft optical axis. We use it for headlines, hero numbers, and the wordmark itself. Settings: opsz 144 for display sizes, soft 50–60 for warmth, weight 320–380. Open-source via Google Fonts. Free for commercial use.

Typographic principles

  • Numbers are typographic. Any meaningful number — a personal best, a recovery score, a target — is set in Fraunces with tabular numerics. Numbers tell stories; we set them the same way we set headlines.
  • Mono for metadata, never for data. Timestamps and labels are mono. The number itself is display. Resist the temptation to mono all numerics.
  • Letter-spacing tightens with size. -0.04em at hero, -0.025em at H2, -0.005em at body, 0 at caption.
  • Optical sizing is on. Both Fraunces and Söhne support optical sizing. Use it.
07 — Iconography

Quiet lines.

Linear, two-pixel weight, rounded terminals, 24-pixel grid. No filled icons except a single state — selected.

Rules

  • Stroke weight: 1.5px on a 24-grid; scales proportionally.
  • Terminals: rounded line-cap, rounded line-join.
  • Corner radius: 2px on rectangles; full circles preferred to rounded squares.
  • No interior fills. Selected state introduces a fill in Moss Soft.
  • One concept per icon. If you need two glyphs to explain, the icon is wrong.
  • Lucide is the starting library; replace any glyph that doesn't match the rules above.
08 — Motion

Motion has a posture.

Cadence's product contains live, real-time, animated data. Motion is therefore a first-class brand element — not decoration. Five principles govern it.

i.

Slow in, quicker out.

Default easing: cubic-bezier(0.2, 0.7, 0.2, 1). Things ease into being and leave decisively. Default duration: 240ms for UI changes, 800–1200ms for first-paint reveals.

ii.

Data updates breathe.

When a number changes — heart rate, recovery, pace — it does not snap. It transitions across 600–900ms with the new value counted up or down. The user should feel the data alive, not refreshing.

iii.

No bounce, no overshoot.

Cadence is not springy. The brand is not springy. No physics-based bounce. Spring easings are forbidden except inside the iOS app where they match platform expectation.

iv.

First paint is a sequence.

On any page load or session start, content arrives in order: structure → labels → data → accents. Each element offset by 60–80ms. The page is composed in front of the user, not delivered all at once.

v.

Voice has motion too.

When the assistant is listening, a soft moss-coloured pulse breathes at 0.4Hz — slower than a resting heartbeat. When the assistant is thinking, the pulse becomes a horizontal line that lengthens and shortens. When the assistant is speaking, the line responds to amplitude. These three states are the entire visual vocabulary of the assistant.

09 — Data Visualisation

The data is the brand.

More than logo, more than colour: the way Cadence displays a heart-rate curve is how the brand is felt every morning. Get this right; the rest matters less.

Categorical palette (data series)

  • D1 — Moss #6B7A5A — primary series, always.
  • D2 — Clay #B96A4D — secondary; comparison or alert.
  • D3 — Slate #4A5F6E — tertiary; baseline / target.
  • D4 — Sand #C9A24E — fourth series only.
  • D5 — Mauve #8A7396 — fifth.
  • D6 — Stone #5F5C55 — historical / faded.
  • D7 — Brick #A3493D — extreme / negative-emphasis.

Rules

  • Never use more than three series on a single chart by default.
  • Reserve the gradient fill for primary series only; comparators are line-only.
  • Grid lines are dashed Stone 100 at 1px. Baselines are solid.
  • Highlight a point with a Moss/Ink dot and a 6px halo. Tooltips appear in a paper card on hover.
  • Axis labels are mono, 10px, Stone 300.
  • The number being displayed is always larger than the chart.

The number is the story.

In Cadence, the chart is supporting evidence for the number, not the other way around. Hero numbers — recovery score, weekly load, resting HR — are set in Fraunces at 64–96px with the unit tucked beside in 14px sans. The chart sits below as confirmation. This inversion is the single most distinctive thing about the data UI.

10 — Tone of Voice

Direct, dry, capable.

The assistant speaks every day. The product UI speaks every minute. Both share the same register — that of a competent personal aide who has known you for some time and does not need to perform.

Cadence sounds like

  • A senior colleague you actually want to work with.
  • Someone who finishes their sentences early.
  • British understatement, but not arch.
  • Confident enough to be brief.
  • Warm enough to use your name occasionally.

Cadence does not sound like

  • A customer-service bot ("How may I assist you today?").
  • A hype-merchant ("Let's crush it!").
  • A friend who is trying too hard ("Hey buddy!").
  • A wellness influencer ("Honour your body's wisdom.").
  • A therapist ("How does that make you feel?").

Word lists

We say

  • session, not workout
  • moved, not rescheduled
  • logged, not tracked
  • recovery, not readiness
  • noted, not understood
  • see you tomorrow, not have a great day

We don't say

  • journey, crush, smash, slay
  • amazing, awesome, fantastic
  • let's, let me, I'm here to help
  • !! or any emoji except in user-generated context
  • "As an AI…" or "I'd be happy to…"
  • literally, basically, actually (as filler)
11 — Sonic Identity

The voice that calls you.

The assistant places real phone calls and answers them. Its voice is the single most-experienced element of the brand. It needs as much care as the wordmark.

The spoken voice

Gender
Either, selected by the user at onboarding. The default voice is neutral-leaning-male, British-English, mid-thirties register.
Accent
Received Pronunciation softened — closer to a clear-voiced South-East English than to a newsreader's BBC. Not estuary, not received.
Pace
Slightly slower than conversational. Around 140 words per minute. Confident pauses; no rushing into the next sentence.
Pitch range
Narrow. The voice does not perform delight or alarm. It modulates within a small band, with intent on the verbs.
Filler
No "um" or "ah" except when the assistant is genuinely thinking. Never used to seem human.
Greeting
Uses the user's first name no more than once per call, at the open or the close. Not in the middle.
Sign-off
"Speak soon." Not "Have a great day." Not "Bye!"

Sound design

Three sounds, no more. Each is a single soft note, no chime cluster.

Incoming call
A two-note rising fifth on a felt piano. 1.8s loop. Warmer than a phone ringtone, cooler than a notification.
Session complete
A single low C, allowed to decay fully (3s). The sound of an ending, not a victory.
Confirmation
A short, soft tap — like a fingertip on wood. 80ms. Used for taps, sends, "done."

What is forbidden, sonically

  • Any "ta-da", any victory fanfare.
  • Synth pads, ambient drones in the background of voice calls.
  • Voice processing that sounds robotic — the assistant uses the highest-quality TTS available (ElevenLabs / OpenAI Voice / equivalent), not a system voice.
  • Music underneath any assistant communication.
12 — Touchpoints

Where the brand lives.

In order of time spent. The brand is built outward from the surface the user touches most.

01.

Web application — primary surface

The dashboard, planner, history, settings, and ambient TV-mode. Designed at 1440px first, scales to 4K for the TV display. Bone background, generous whitespace, Fraunces for hero numbers. The TV-mode defaults to dark and is composed at-a-glance — no controls, no interactivity, no chrome.

02.

iOS application — secondary surface

Voice-first, optimised for quick capture: a tap to start a session, a held button to talk to the assistant, a single-line journal entry. Uses iOS native components (SF Symbols replaced with Cadence icons; SF Pro replaced with Söhne). Haptic feedback on every confirmation.

03.

Assistant voice — sonic surface

The voice on the call. See §11. This is where the brand lives most viscerally and where its quality is most easily eroded by a single bad TTS choice. Treat as if it were a typeface.

04.

Future — marketing site

When the product opens to the public, the marketing site is a single page. A hero number. A line of Fraunces. A short loop of the ambient display. No screenshots in fake browser chrome. No testimonials in a carousel. No "trusted by" logo wall. The product is the marketing.

05.

Future — App Store presence

The icon is the monogram on a moss field. The screenshots are the product, photographed at 3x, no annotations. The description is six lines.

The product is the marketing. The data is the brand. The voice is the soul.
